\chapter*{Abstract}

The corpus everything in this work is measured against is Salishan speech, written down. This research paper
says whose words it holds, how wrong it could be, and how that was measured.

The corpus is a glossed word list: each form is taken out of a published paper and paired with its
meaning, and the pairs are the research's own data. Every table opens with the person who spoke,
before the linguist who published and before anyone who transcribed the paper into a table.
Twenty-five tables carry 16,822 rows. Sixteen name their speakers. Nine cite a published source and
name no speaker, and those say so instead of guessing. A linguist wrote the paper and a person transcribed it, and
neither of those is whose language it is.

How wrong the corpus could be is stated as an upper bound, never as a measurement of the rate.
Three channels fail for different reasons: a form written that the paper does not hold, a token the
paper prints that no row holds, and a token the corpus lost on the way out of an automatic extractor.
The checks observe 163 failures in 20,261 trials, 28 in 7,440, and 4 in 9,430. Taken as independent
the joint bound is $1.28 \times 10^{-8}$ per line. The derivation says where that independence is
thin, and the largest unmodelled term is that one person transcribed every table.

The method finds a border it is never shown. Mellesmoen and Kye label every form they cite as
northern or southern Lushootseed, a fact a linguist published. Given the forms and never the labels,
holding the concept fixed through the word web and flattening the pooled run counts to maximum
entropy, the term that carries the border comes back as the stressed schwa, southern, at 45 against 6
over the same concepts, matched by 1 of 200 random borders.

Two papers here are set in a font with no Unicode map. Their extracted text is the font's encoding
and not the page. Their page texts are drafted rule by rule and remain drafts: a rule that guesses at
labialization is named as guessing. Five more papers hold none of their own orthography in their
text layer, and none of them is counted for or against the corpus.

These methods read a language and can put one back. Near the center of a subject a regeneration is
a copy, and far from it the output is obvious nonsense. Between them is a narrow band where the
output is coherent and may already not be the language, and nothing here marks which side of it a
result fell on. That question belongs to a native speaker, and no amount of measurement turns it
into one an algorithm can answer.
