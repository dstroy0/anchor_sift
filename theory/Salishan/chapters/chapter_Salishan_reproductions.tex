\chapter{Reproducing what the linguists claimed}

\section{Nater on Bella Coola}

\textbf{Purpose:} Take a claim a linguist published about one of these languages, and find out
whether the corpus on disk reproduces it. \textbf{Scope:}
\texttt{build/\allowbreak{}experiments/\allowbreak{}nater\_\allowbreak{}bella\_\allowbreak{}coola.\allowbreak{}py},
the Nuxalk corpora under \texttt{build/\allowbreak{}corpora/\allowbreak{}}.

\textbf{Margaret Siwallace} told the tale this is measured over. Hank Nater wrote it down and
published it, and a person read it into a table for this work. The language is hers.

\subsection{Why this is a different kind of test}

Almost every reading in this work is scored against the corpus it came out of. Section 7 of
\texttt{corpus-\allowbreak{}derivation} is the exception that mattered most: a border a linguist
published, held out, and recovered. This is the second of that kind and it is cheaper, because the
answer was printed in a table forty years ago and nothing here can move it.

Nater's ICSNL59 paper, \emph{Voiceless Words in Bella Coola: Fact vs. Fiction}, disputes Mellesmoen
(2021) on four points. Its Table 1 prints the Bella Coola phoneme inventory with a per centum
distribution across the entire morpheme inventory, from Nater (1984:27). Thirty-four phonemes with
a percentage each. Transcribed off the page they sum to 99.98, which is how the transcription is
checked: a table with a row dropped still looks like a table, and one that sums to 100 does not have
a row missing.

That table is an oracle in the strict sense this tree uses. It is a measurement somebody else made,
over a sample this work has never seen, and no reading taken here can adjust it.

\subsection{What had to be thrown out first}

Three Nuxalk corpora sit in \texttt{build/\allowbreak{}corpora/\allowbreak{}}. Two of them cannot
carry this measurement and neither says so in its name.

\textbf{One is nearly a tenth English.} The purity control is a word list built from
\texttt{cc\_\allowbreak{}english.\allowbreak{}txt}, four letters and up, which is already in the tree
for the sift's own anchor. \texttt{How Salish is Bella Coola} scores 169 English tokens in 1934, at
0.087: \emph{introduction}, \emph{however}, \emph{cognates}, \emph{database}. The other two score
0.018 and 0.008.

The mark set is not the test used here, and using it would have been the obvious mistake. A mark set
calls a token the language when it carries one of the characters the language is written with, and
Ayajuthem and St'át'imcets are written in practical orthographies that use none of them. Asked that
way, Mary George's corpus reads 0.639 foreign and Carl Alexander's 0.602, when both are the
language. An English word list has the opposite failure and it is the harmless one: it can only
accuse a token of being English, and a Bella Coola word is not in an English dictionary.

\textbf{One is Nater's own cluster charts.} The pure stream for the voiceless-words paper holds
\texttt{ck}, \texttt{cp}, \texttt{cq}, \texttt{kƛ'i}: those are the entries of his Tables 2 to 5,
lifted out of the paper by its reader. Counting phonemes over that file counts an obstruent cluster
inventory as though it were speech, and pooling it with the narrative inflated every obstruent while
leaving \texttt{a} five per cent short.

It is told from speech by measuring it. The tale runs 8.50 phonemes to a word with 0.954 of its
words carrying a vowel; the chart runs under three phonemes with under a third of its entries
vowelled. That is the shape of a chart and not of a language, and the check is in the script rather
than in a list of filenames, so the next chart to arrive is caught by the same test.

What is left is Margaret Siwallace's tale: 217 words, 644 phonemes read as a morpheme inventory,
31 characters no phoneme accounts for.

\subsection{The reproduction}

Counted as a morpheme inventory, which is what Nater counted, the tale sits at a total variation of
0.169 from his table.

Three of his rows read exactly zero in the corpus: the vocalic sonorants \texttt{m̩}, \texttt{n̩} and
\texttt{l̩}. The corpus does not write the syllabicity diacritic and spells \texttt{m̩} as
\texttt{m}. That is a fact about the extraction and not about Bella Coola, and it puts 3.58 per cent
of the published table in cells the corpus can never fill while inflating the three plain sonorants
by the same mass.

Folding his syllabic rows into their plain counterparts is a correction to the oracle, which is the
one move that would make this worthless if it were made quietly. Made openly, it predicts where the
three plain sonorants should land, and they land there:

\subsection{Whether 0.136 is anything}

A total variation is a number until it has a floor under it and a wrong answer beside it.

\textbf{The floor.} The tale is split by alternating words, which samples it instead of cutting it
in place, and the two halves are measured against each other. That is the resolution of this count
at 644 phonemes: \textbf{0.066}. The reading sits at about twice its own floor, so there is a real
gap between this corpus and the published table and it is not all noise.

\textbf{The wrong answer.} The same count is taken over every other Salishan corpus on disk and
scored against Nater's Bella Coola table. Fourteen of them are large enough to ask.

That is a positive control on the corpus and not on the method. What it says is that the extraction,
the reader and the three checks preserved enough of Bella Coola for a frequency count to find its
way home.

\subsection{The claim about nuclei}

Mellesmoen's Good Enough Nucleus Hypothesis, as Nater quotes it, holds that every word has at least
one syllable and that anything equal or greater in sonority to a fricative may stand in the nuclear
position. Nater disputes the exclusion of stops, cites all-stop words and roots, and concludes with
Newman (1947) that Bella Coola is essentially non-syllabic.

Whether such words are in this corpus is a count. In Margaret Siwallace's tale, \textbf{17 distinct
morphemes carry no sonorant and no vowel}: \texttt{ck}, \texttt{cx}, \texttt{c'kt}, \texttt{kł},
\texttt{kʷc}, \texttt{kʷƛ'}, \texttt{k'x}, \texttt{skʷ'}, \texttt{sx}, \texttt{tx}, \texttt{tχ},
\texttt{tχʷ}, \texttt{t'xʷ}, \texttt{xł}, \texttt{xʷc} among them. One of the 17 is built from stops
alone: \texttt{kʷkʷ'}.

The cluster chart answers this question 103 times over and the answer is worth nothing, because
every entry in it is an obstruent cluster by construction. Reciting the table a claim was argued
from is not evidence for the claim. The narrative is the only line here that bears on it, and one
short tale is what it rests on.

This is a count and not an argument about syllable theory, which nothing here reaches. What can be
said is that voiceless words are in the running speech on disk, at 17 in a single tale, so they are
not an artifact of citation lists.

\subsection{What this cannot do}

\textbf{The areal claim is out of reach.} Nater's fourth point is that long obstruent clustering is
shared with Oowekyala, at Rivers Inlet, and is therefore areal rather than inherited. Oowekyala is
Wakashan and there is no Oowekyala on disk. The test has a shape: the same phoneme count over an
Oowekyala corpus, against Bella Coola and against the Salishan corpora, with the question being
whether Bella Coola sits nearer its neighbour than its family. Nothing here can run it until that
corpus exists, and saying so is the whole of what can be said.

\textbf{The residual is not explained.} After the fold, \texttt{s} is 3.32 low, \texttt{ʔ} 2.01
high, \texttt{u} 2.36 high and \texttt{kʷ} 1.90 high. \texttt{s} is the most frequent obstruent in
the language and one 217-word tale is a thin sample of it. Whether that gap is the sample, the
genre, or a difference between Nater's morpheme inventory and any running narrative is not decided
here, and more Bella Coola narrative is what would decide it.

\textbf{A morpheme inventory built from marked boundaries is not the one Nater had.} His was a
lexicographer's list. This one is what the corpus marks with a hyphen and a shelf, deduplicated.
They are the same kind of object and they are not the same object.
