\chapter{Why a game}

\textbf{Purpose:} Say what a game supplies that no other corpus in this work does, and why that is
about cost and not about games. \textbf{Scope:} \texttt{examples/\allowbreak{}games/\allowbreak{}},
\texttt{src/\allowbreak{}engine/\allowbreak{}python/\allowbreak{}representation/\allowbreak{}game/\allowbreak{}}

\section{The hole this fills is named in the workbook}

The workbook divides its own claims in two and the division is not a matter of confidence. One half
is proved: a subset of a pattern's points is a necessary condition, the maximum entropy background
under a fixed constraint is unique, a null permutation is distribution-free. Nothing about a corpus
bears on any of it, and a hundred agreeing corpora carry the weight of one because the property was
in doubt on none of them.

The other half is reach, and the workbook says plainly what closing it would take. A claim covering
a whole kind of object is empirical, quantified over a set nobody has enumerated, and no run of any
length settles it. An eleventh row raises a count and moves the quantifier no distance. What moves
it is a controlled series inside one domain, with an outside answer attached to every row, and the
workbook prices that at years because obtaining the rows is the expense.

That price is a fact about the domains this work has read and not about the argument. A crystal costs
a database and a fetch. A protein costs the same. A corpus of Salishan speech costs papers held
under terms, transcription by hand, and an obligation to the people whose language it is that no
amount of arithmetic discharges. In every one of them the reading is cheap and the row is dear.

\section{What a game costs}

A game inverts that. The rules of an impartial game are a few lines, the position space is generated
by those lines, and the answer each position carries is a theorem.

Take a subtraction game: a heap of \(n\), a finite set \(S\) of legal takes, and whoever cannot move
loses. The Grundy value of a position is the least non-negative integer absent from the values of
the positions it reaches, which is four lines of code, and the sequence of those values is
eventually periodic. That last is not an observation. It is proved, and the period is a quantity a
separate routine finds by sliding the tail against itself and checking equality to the end.

So a row of this series is: choose a move set, generate the sequence, compute the exact period by
comparison, hand the sequence to the detector, compare. The generator and the answer share no code
with the detector, the answer is exact and carries no tolerance, and the number of rows is limited
by patience rather than by budget. Three hundred and eighty five move sets took under a minute.

The point is not that games are interesting. The point is that this is the first domain in the work
where the controlled series the workbook asks for can actually be run, and the workbook's estimate
of years was an estimate about corpora that have to be obtained.

\section{Two kinds of object, and they are not the same evidence}

This book reads both and keeps them apart.

An \textbf{impartial game} is a position index carrying a value. There is no board in it, no player,
and no choice: the value at a position is determined by the rules alone. This is where the series
lives, and where the two old theorems sit.

A \textbf{played board} is a position: a square is a point and the piece on it is its value. A board
carries two ingredients that every other corpus in this work holds welded together. A text carries
its grammar and its author at once and there is no way to obtain the same author under a different
grammar. A board separates them for nothing, because three players of different strength on one
fixed rule set give nine corpora differing in the play alone.

That separation is the played half's whole reason to exist, and what it returned is a negative. The
measure reads the rules and not the play. That is recorded in its own chapter with the control that
established it.

\section{What is not claimed}

A game is not a natural object. Its rules were written by a person and its regularity was put there
on purpose, which makes it a positive control and not a discovery. The crystallography chapter makes
the same admission about a published cell edge and it applies here with less excuse, since nobody
had to go and measure a subtraction game.

What a positive control is for is the one thing a memoryless control cannot do. A memoryless corpus
returning 1.00 shows an instrument does not invent structure. It cannot show the instrument finds
structure that is there. These rows show that, cheaply, at a count that makes a coincidence
untenable, and they show something a memoryless control could never have shown: where a reading is
confident, clears every floor, and is still not what it is called.
