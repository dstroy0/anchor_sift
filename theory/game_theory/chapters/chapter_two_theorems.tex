\section{Bouton, 1901}

Nim is heaps, a move takes any number from one heap, and whoever cannot move loses. Bouton showed
that a position loses for the player to move exactly when the heap sizes exclusive or to zero.

One consequence of that is readable by counting and needs no exclusive or performed by the reader.
Fix every heap but one. Exactly one size of the free heap makes the total zero, so every axis line of
the grid holds exactly one losing position.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrll@{}}
\toprule
game & lines & losses per line & loss density \\
\midrule
2 heaps of 64 & 64 & \{1\} & 0.01562 \\
3 heaps of 64 & 4096 & \{1\} & 0.01562 \\
4 heaps of 32 & 32768 & \{1\} & 0.03125 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The set is one and not a range, on every line, in every arity. A set of the same density scattered
over the same cells puts exactly one loss in a line of 64 with probability 0.371, so a scatter
producing all ones over four thousand and ninety six lines is not something to quantify past saying
it does not happen.

This grid is generated from Bouton's own theorem, which is stated in the module and is why these
rows are reported as a count and not as a recovery. What they establish is that the property is in
the grid where a reader can reach it, without arithmetic that knows what Nim is.

\section{Wythoff, 1907}

Wythoff's game is two heaps, where a move takes any number from one heap or the same number from
both. Wythoff showed the losing positions are the pairs
\(\left(\lfloor n\varphi \rfloor, \lfloor n\varphi^{2} \rfloor\right)\) and their mirrors.

The grid here is played out by the minimum excludant recursion and holds no formula, so the
comparison against that closed form is a check and not a definition.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrlrl@{}}
\toprule
side & positions & matches the 1907 form & \(a_n/n\) & relative error \\
\midrule
200 & 76 & yes & 1.605263 & \(7.89\times10^{-3}\) \\
400 & 152 & yes & 1.611842 & \(3.83\times10^{-3}\) \\
800 & 305 & yes & 1.616393 & \(1.01\times10^{-3}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every pair agrees with the published one, at every side. The second coordinate minus the first runs
1, 2, 3, 4 and onward with no exception, which is the other half of Wythoff's statement. And the
ratio converges on \(\varphi = 1.618034\) from below, reaching one part in a thousand at a side of
800, which is a published irrational arriving out of a game played by a recursion that was told
nothing.

\section{Why the pair is here together}

They are the same shape of game and their answers are opposite in the one way this work cares about.

Nim's losing set is built from the exclusive or, which is a bitwise operation, and its regularity is
exact at every scale. Wythoff's sits on a line of slope \(\varphi\), and a line of irrational slope
passes through no two lattice points in the same relation twice. Nothing on it repeats, not
approximately and not eventually.

So the pair is a positive and a negative control with published answers on both sides, which is
something this work has not had before. The crystallography chapter argues that a memoryless control
can only show an instrument does not invent structure, and that showing it finds structure that is
there needs an answer from outside. Nim supplies that answer for nothing. Wythoff supplies the other
one: an object with real structure that a shift detector must not report a period on.

The detector reports one. That is the next chapter and it is the reason this book exists.
