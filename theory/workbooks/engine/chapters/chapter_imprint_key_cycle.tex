\chapter{\texorpdfstring{Impulse response, kernel, cycle}{Impulse response, kernel, cycle}}
\label{chap:imprint-key-cycle}

\textbf{Purpose:} State why the engine spends a program's serial work once and then only zips it over a set. A reader can then read the impulse response, the kernel and the cycle as one picture and know which parts a proof already holds.

\textbf{Scope:} \texttt{engine/\allowbreak{}base/\allowbreak{}keymath/\allowbreak{}}, \texttt{engine/\allowbreak{}base/\allowbreak{}key\_\allowbreak{}schedule/\allowbreak{}}, \texttt{engine/\allowbreak{}base/\allowbreak{}cycle/\allowbreak{}}, and the residual program \texttt{residual\_\allowbreak{}program} (\texttt{engine/\allowbreak{}base/\allowbreak{}residual/\allowbreak{}}) states and whose impulse response \texttt{engine\_\allowbreak{}residual} in \texttt{engine/\allowbreak{}engine.\allowbreak{}cu} computes.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The atom}{The atom}}

Atom is the storage class and what it holds: all of the smallest thing under inspection, at whatever scale that thing is. A frame can be an atom, a letter, a part of a cell, a literal atom, a basketball. In the residual it is one frame. Its lanes are its sixteen bit samples in raster order, lane i at place 2\textasciicircum{}(16 i) (Kronecker substitution). The frame is then one exact integer, the stored stack is that integer's limbs, and nothing is converted to hold it.

\section{\texorpdfstring{1. The impulse response: transitivity makes a chain one thing}{1. The impulse response: transitivity makes a chain one thing}}

A step carries a value to a value. Steps chain transitively: if T1 carries a to b and T2 carries b to c, then T2∘T1 carries a to c, and composition does not care how the chain is bracketed:

T3(T2(T1(atom))) = (T3∘T2∘T1)(atom)

So the chain can be composed first, on a stand-in, and applied after. The stand-in is the impulse: the null holding one unit. For a linear, shift-invariant step, whatever the chain does to the impulse is everything it does to any atom:

T(atom) = T(impulse) * atom

The chain's response to the impulse is its \textbf{kernel}, its impulse response. Computing it is pushing the impulse through the program once. A binomial smoothing of order n is n unit steps [1 1]. The impulse comes out as Pascal's row, by construction and not from a table. The residual's program is: smooth narrow, keep, smooth wide, then 2\textasciicircum{}g times the kept value less the running one. Its impulse response is

kernel = 2\textasciicircum{}g * B\_\allowbreak{}narrow  -  B\_\allowbreak{}wide * B\_\allowbreak{}narrow

That is two separable terms, each one exact row per axis. For the orders asked of it so far the kernel's weights reach about 2\textasciicircum{}126. They are exact integers, as wide as they grow.

\textbf{What transitivity requires.} a→b and b→c give a→c only when the b that T1 hands over is the same b that T2 takes in. Every step has to be closed and exact: its output lands in the same exact set, at the width the next step reads, with nothing rounded, truncated or wrapped at the hand-off. With 64 bit rounding, (a∘b)∘c and a∘(b∘c) differ, and the computed kernel would not be the chain. With exact integers they are the same number. The impulse response sizes every lane to its proven bound for this reason: a lane below 2\textasciicircum{}m under a row summing to S stays below 2\textasciicircum{}(m + bits(S−1)).

\section{\texorpdfstring{2. Shift-and-add: a kernel is a pattern of masks}{2. Shift-and-add: a kernel is a pattern of masks}}

Multiplying by a constant is a series of masks. n·4 is n shifted by 2, and n·5 is (n shifted by 2) plus n. A kernel weight w applied to a lane x is

w · x = sum over the set bits b of w of (x shifted by b)

Each set bit of the kernel selects one shifted copy of the array, and applying the kernel is ANDing that pattern against the atom and summing what it selects. Deriving the pattern is the expensive part: every serial step, and every costly operation in the program. It happens once, on the impulse. After that each atom costs one application of the kernel, not one pass per step.

Kernels compose into kernels: the kernel of A then B is B's kernel applied to A's kernel. Two operations composed this way are therefore one kernel again, and a chain of any length costs every later atom the same as one operation. That is the compression of time: the chain's cost is amortized over the set. Because a composed kernel is itself a step, it can be a step in another program. That is the recursion engine two runs on a cfg from the scheduler.

\textbf{Order of operations is enforced.} Composition regroups freely (it is associative) but does not reorder freely: it is not commutative once pointwise steps and linear steps are mixed. The scheduler composes kernels only in program order, and reorders two steps only where it is proven they commute. Proven means, for example, two centered symmetric rows under the same mirror fold.

The same holds for the atom read as an integer. With lanes L bits wide, the atom is A = Σ v\_\allowbreak{}i 2\textasciicircum{}(L i) and the kernel is an integer K placed at the same strides. When L is wide enough that no lane carries into the next, the lanes of A·K are the smoothed values. \textbf{The residual of a whole frame is one product of two exact integers.} A separable kernel factors as K = K\_\allowbreak{}z · K\_\allowbreak{}y · K\_\allowbreak{}x, and by associativity the product is three sparse multiplications, one per axis. Those are the cycle's three sweeps.

Anything periodic is precomputed the same way. Modulo is a wave: once one period is known, the rest is a shift. The mirror fold at the edges repeats with period twice the line: one period per axis is laid down once as a table of offsets, and every tap reads its offset there. A lane's digits z y x repeat with the lane: a thread divides once for its first lane, and after that adds the grid stride's own digits with carries.

\section{\texorpdfstring{3. Run it over the set continuously}{3. Run it over the set continuously}}

The kernel does not depend on the atom, and computing the impulse response fixes every lane width before any atom is seen. Nothing about the set needs a second look. The set streams through:

\begin{itemize}
\item One launch covers every lane of every atom in a slab.
\item The grid is held at a barrier between the sweeps.
\item The last sweep folds every term into each output lane in two's complement.
\end{itemize}

The kernel stays resident, the atoms flow past it, and the output of one slab is the input the next stage reads while the cycle moves on.

\textbf{Nothing is agglomerated.} Arithmetic is exact, discrimination is unlimited, and the machine does not halt. There is no error in the traditional sense to average away. No step merges, clusters, averages or rounds a lane toward its neighbors. Every tap's product accumulates into exact columns and the whole sum is kept.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Where the noise sits}{Where the noise sits}}

The wide background term is the medium's own scattering, its turbidity. Nephelometry across a dilution series reads that turbidity as the fluid's mean entropic flux, and that whole number is noise to us. The kernel subtracts it whole. What the residual keeps is only structure standing above the medium.

\section{\texorpdfstring{What a proof holds}{What a proof holds}}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Impulse response.} The residual's impulse response is computed once, as two terms. Its lanes need 286 bits and are held in 9 limbs (288).
\item \textbf{Exact against the old residual.} Over all 25 samples, every lane of every frame was compared with the residual the smoothing passes and the transform computed. 0 of 10,485,760,000 lanes differ, over 2,500 frames. The passes and the transform are gone, and \texttt{engine\_\allowbreak{}residual} now runs the same kernel.
\item \textbf{Speed.} 31 ms a frame, against 24.5 ms for the passes it replaced. The next saving is the scheduler's: keep the binomial kernel factored as [1 1]\textasciicircum{}n. A sweep is then neighbor additions with no multiplies, and the shared factor B\_\allowbreak{}narrow is computed once for both terms.
\item \textbf{Built: the lookup-table step.} A pointwise step is precomputed on its alphabet: every value a lane can hold, pushed through once, and the table holds the results (M10 and A13 of \hyperref[chap:engine-table]{engine\_\allowbreak{}table.md}). The request carries the table (\texttt{Engine\allowbreak{}Record\allowbreak{}Table}, an index of 1 to 32 bits); keymath refuses a table wider than its source (\texttt{keymath.\allowbreak{}cu}), the scheduler places it (\texttt{key\_\allowbreak{}schedule.\allowbreak{}cu}) and the cycle reads it (\texttt{cycle.\allowbreak{}cu}, \texttt{ENGINE\_\allowbreak{}RECORD\_\allowbreak{}TABLE}). \texttt{test/\allowbreak{}engine/\allowbreak{}record\_\allowbreak{}table\_\allowbreak{}test} proves x² and |x − 30000| + 100 as tables, on 4,096 lanes of the 16-bit alphabet, device and host (\texttt{build/\allowbreak{}20260923\_\allowbreak{}204007\_\allowbreak{}record\_\allowbreak{}table\_\allowbreak{}test}, 15 checks, 0 failed). Division is built as exact record operations, not as a table: quotient, remainder, gcd and exact quotient, operations 11 to 14 (\texttt{test/\allowbreak{}engine/\allowbreak{}record\_\allowbreak{}divide\_\allowbreak{}test}, \texttt{test/\allowbreak{}engine/\allowbreak{}exact\_\allowbreak{}divide\_\allowbreak{}test}). Division as a table is untested. Square root is not in the engine and is untested. No mask generator is built: the caller fills the table.
\end{itemize}
